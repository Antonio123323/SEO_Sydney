How HMRC Tracks Crypto in the UK: CARF, Exchange Data and Records

Copied

HMRC crypto tracking is a practical concern for people who buy, sell or move digital assets through UK and overseas services. The central point is simple: a blockchain transaction, an exchange statement and a tax return are different records, but together they may help HM Revenue & Customs (HMRC) understand cryptoasset activity.

This guide explains what the UK Cryptoasset Reporting Framework (CARF) changes in 2026, which crypto exchanges report to HMRC under the rules, what a provider report can and cannot establish, and what to prepare if HMRC asks questions. It is an educational guide, not personalised tax advice.

Does HMRC Track Crypto Transactions in the UK?

HMRC can examine cryptoasset activity using information obtained from service providers, taxpayer records and publicly accessible blockchain data. A visible wallet transaction does not automatically identify its owner, but exchange records, account details and transaction histories may help connect activity to an individual.

The question “do crypto exchanges report to HMRC?” now has a clearer regulatory answer for providers within CARF. However, reporting coverage depends on the provider, jurisdiction, applicable rules and reporting period. It would be inaccurate to say that every exchange sends HMRC every transaction in real time.

Understand UK Crypto Tax Reporting

What Changed Under CARF in 2026?

The Cryptoasset Reporting Framework introduced due-diligence and reporting requirements for in-scope UK reporting cryptoasset service providers from 1 January 2026. Providers may need to collect identifying information about users and report relevant transaction data. These obligations concern providers; they do not replace the user’s responsibility to assess and report their own tax position.

The first reporting period runs from 1 January to 31 December 2026. HMRC states that the first provider reports must be submitted between 1 January and 31 May 2027. Later reports are generally due by 31 May for the preceding calendar year. A calendar-year CARF report is not the same as a UK individual’s April-to-April tax year.

The 2026 UK crypto tax crackdown is a phrase used in searches and commentary, not the formal name of CARF. More systematic data reporting can support compliance checks, but it does not prove that every user owes tax.

Which Crypto Exchanges Report to HMRC?

When asking which crypto exchanges report to HMRC, begin with whether the business is a reporting cryptoasset service provider under the applicable rules. The category can include exchanges, brokers and dealers that facilitate cryptoasset transactions. A brand name or a UK-facing website alone is not enough to establish the legal reporting position of every group company.

Under CARF, UK reporting providers must assess their reporting obligations. In some circumstances, information concerning UK residents using non-UK providers may also reach HMRC through international tax-information exchange arrangements. This depends on the jurisdiction and rules in force, so a universal list of exchanges that “always report” would be misleading.

If a platform requests tax residence, a tax identification number or other self-certification information, check its official notices and privacy documentation. Do not treat an identity request as proof that a particular transaction was taxable.

What Information May HMRC Receive?

Under the reporting framework, providers collect identifying details and relevant transaction information. The exact fields and reporting treatment depend on the applicable CARF guidance. Information may help HMRC associate account activity with a tax record; it does not, by itself, calculate a person’s final taxable gain.

For example, a platform might show a disposal value, while the taxpayer’s acquisition costs, matching rules, fees and transfers between personally controlled wallets need separate analysis. HMRC explicitly warns that exchange transaction reports are not tax calculations and may not track pooled costs.

Which Crypto Activities Can Create a Tax Question?

For individuals, selling tokens for pounds, exchanging one token for another, paying for goods or services with tokens, and certain gifts can count as disposals for Capital Gains Tax purposes. Receiving cryptoassets from employment, mining, staking or other activities can raise Income Tax questions, depending on the facts.

Moving tokens between wallets that you beneficially own is generally not a disposal merely because the tokens move on-chain. Keep evidence showing that both addresses were under your control, particularly where an exchange statement makes the transfer look like an outgoing payment.

Consider this illustrative GBP example: someone acquires tokens for £1,200 and later disposes of them for £1,550. The simple difference is £350 before any relevant allowable costs, matching adjustments or other tax considerations. This is not necessarily the taxable gain or the tax due. The purpose is to show why an exchange’s gross transaction figure cannot replace a full calculation.

What Records Should You Keep for HMRC?

Keep dated transaction exports from each exchange, wallet transaction references, token quantities, sterling values at relevant times, fees, bank statements and evidence of acquisition costs. Where appropriate, retain records that connect transfers between your own wallets and show the pooled cost history used in calculations.

HMRC guidance places responsibility for record-keeping on the taxpayer. Exchange statements can be incomplete, and an account may become inaccessible later. Download records periodically and store backups securely; do not place recovery phrases or private keys in tax spreadsheets.

For custody and wallet terminology, see our separate guide to crypto wallet custody and records in the UK. It explains wallet types without treating wallet storage itself as a tax calculation.

What Happens During an HMRC Crypto Tax Enquiry?

An HMRC crypto tax enquiry or compliance check may involve a request for records that explain particular transactions, valuations or reported figures. The appropriate response depends on the letter, the tax year and the person’s circumstances. Preserve the original communication, check it through official HMRC channels and note any stated response deadline.

Prepare a chronological audit trail rather than assuming that a single exchange CSV answers every question. Reconcile exchange exports, personal wallets, GBP valuations, bank movements and tax-return entries. Where records are missing or a historic return may be incorrect, consider seeking advice from a suitably qualified UK tax professional.

The phrase HMRC crypto tax crackdown should not be read as an announcement that all crypto users are under investigation. Reporting, routine compliance checks and individual enquiries are distinct processes.

How to Avoid Common Reporting Mistakes

Do not assume that an offshore exchange makes activity invisible, or that a wallet-to-wallet transfer is necessarily a taxable sale. Equally, do not assume that every on-chain movement is tax-free: ownership changes, token swaps and other arrangements require their own analysis.

Do not confuse the provider’s CARF deadline in May 2027 with a deadline for your personal Self Assessment return. For a fuller discussion of reporting obligations and calculations, consult the dedicated tax guide rather than using a data-sharing article as a substitute.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can HMRC see my crypto wallet?

Public blockchain transactions can be viewed, but identifying the person controlling an address may require other evidence. HMRC can also ask for relevant records during a compliance check.

Do crypto exchanges report to HMRC automatically?

In-scope providers have reporting duties under CARF, with the first UK reporting period covering 2026 and first submissions due by 31 May 2027. This is not a claim that all platforms report all transactions immediately.

Does receiving a CARF information request mean I owe tax?

No. Identity and tax-residence checks help a provider meet reporting rules. Tax liability depends on the actual activity and applicable tax rules.

Can I rely only on exchange tax summaries?

No. HMRC notes that exchange reports are not complete tax calculations and do not necessarily maintain pooled costs.

What if HMRC contacts me about past crypto activity?

Verify the communication, gather records and consider professional help where the facts or earlier returns are complex.
